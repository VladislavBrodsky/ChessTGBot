# Free A.I. Chess Practice: A Three-Game Threat Drill

*Give each practice game one job: notice a threat, test a response, and review the result.*

Category: Product · 3 min read · Draft for review

A free game against an A.I. opponent is most useful when you give it a job. Do not try to improve every part of your chess at once. Pick one decision you want to notice, play, and check whether you actually noticed it. A win can hide a missed threat; a loss can still contain a good decision.

## Set one question before game one

Try this question: what did my opponent's last move attack? Before each of your moves, name one newly attacked piece or pawn. If nothing new is attacked, say so and look for checks and captures. The aim is to practise a repeatable scan, not to claim an engine-approved best move.

## A position to rehearse

Play through 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5 4.c3 Nf6. It is White to move. Pause before looking for a move: Black's knight has arrived on f6 and now attacks the pawn on e4. What would you do about that attack?

**Position:** Constructed opening exercise after 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5 4.c3 Nf6. White to move: identify the threat to e4.

`r1bqk2r/pppp1ppp/2n2n2/2b1p3/2B1P3/2P2N2/PP1P1PPP/RNBQK2R w KQkq - 1 5`

One legal answer is 5.d3: the pawn from d2 reaches d3 and defends e4. Another legal choice is 5.d4, which challenges the centre and asks a different question of Black. This exercise tests whether you saw the threat and can explain your choice. It does not claim either move is the strongest engine continuation.

## Use three games, not three random goals

- Game one — notice: choose a comfortable A.I. difficulty. After each opposing move, name the new threat before touching a piece.
- Game two — compare: keep the same difficulty. Before one important move, write down two legal candidates and the reply you expect to each.
- Game three — repeat: look for the same threat pattern. If it does not appear, still record one position where your scan changed your decision.

## Review the decision, then adjust difficulty

After each game, save one position or write its move number. Ask: what changed on the previous move, what did I consider, and what did the opponent actually play? If you missed the attack, replay that moment before starting another game. If you noticed it consistently, choose a harder level or a new question. Do not use a single practice result as a measure of your rating or future match outcome.
