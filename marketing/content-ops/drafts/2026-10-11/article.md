# How to Review a Blitz Game: One Losing Move in Five Minutes

*Draft for review — intended date: 2026-10-11; not published.*

Find one decision you can improve, then carry a short threat check into your next game.

A blitz loss can feel like a blur. You do not need to explain every move to learn from it. Find one decision where a simple threat check would have changed your choice, replay the position, and write down the cue you want to notice next time. This lesson gives you two positions to practise on and a five-minute review you can use after your own game.


## The three questions

- What did their last move change? Look at the piece that just moved, the line it opened and the squares it now attacks.
- What of mine is loose? Find pieces that are undefended or defended only once, and check your king's neighbouring squares.
- After my move, what checks and captures does my opponent get? Imagine the move played, then list every check, every capture and every threat in reply.

Question one looks at what has just happened. Question two checks your vulnerable pieces and king. Question three tests the move you are about to make. With little time left, shorten the routine, but still check the opponent's most forcing reply before a recapture or an attractive attacking move.

The order matters. If you start with your own move, you will fall in love with it before you have looked at anything else, and every later question becomes a search for reasons to keep it. Starting with their move forces you to look at the board as it is, not as you hope it is.


## Worked example one: the queen on h5

Take a well-known opening position. After 1.e4 e5 2.Bc4 Nc6 3.Qh5, it is Black's turn.

**Position.** After 3.Qh5. Black to move. What has White's last move changed?

`r1bqkbnr/pppp1ppp/2n5/4p2Q/2B1P3/8/PPPP1PPP/RNB1K1NR b KQkq - 3 3`

SAN line: `e4 e5 Bc4 Nc6 Qh5`

Question one: White's queen travelled from d1 to h5. From there it attacks the pawn on e5 and the pawn on f7. Question two: the pawn on e5 is guarded by the knight on c6, but f7 is guarded only by the king, and White's bishop on c4 aims at it as well. So there is a real threat: Qxf7#.

Now test a natural reply with question three. 3...Nf6 attacks the queen, which feels like an answer. Yet after 3...Nf6 White has a capture that is also checkmate.

**Position.** After 3...Nf6?? 4.Qxf7#. The knight attacked the queen and defended nothing.

`r1bqkb1r/pppp1Qpp/2n2n2/4p3/2B1P3/8/PPPP1PPP/RNB1K1NR b KQkq - 0 4`

SAN line: `e4 e5 Bc4 Nc6 Qh5 Nf6 Qxf7#`

The knight move attacked something; it did not defend anything. The routine exposes the difference. A move that answers a threat must remove it or defend the target. Black's usual answers include 3...Qe7, which guards f7 directly, or 3...g6, which attacks the queen with a pawn and forces it to leave h5. After either move, White has no mate in one.


## Worked example two: the free pawn on d2

Now a position where the danger sits inside your own move. Black to play, and a pawn on d2 looks ready to be collected.

**Position.** Black to move. 1...Rxd2 wins a pawn. Run question three before you play it.

`3r2k1/5ppp/8/8/8/8/3P1PPP/R5K1 b - - 0 1`

Question one: White's rook stands on a1, with an open file straight to a8. Question two: Black's king has no luft, because the pawns on f7, g7 and h7 seal it in, and the rook on d8 is the only piece guarding the back rank. Question three: after 1...Rxd2, what checks does White have? The rook has left the eighth rank, so 2.Ra8+ comes with force. Black can only interpose 2...Rd8, and then 3.Rxd8# is mate.

**Position.** After 1...Rxd2 2.Ra8+ Rd8 3.Rxd8#. The pawn was bait for the only defender.

`3R2k1/5ppp/8/8/8/8/5PPP/6K1 b - - 0 3`

SAN line: `Rxd2 Ra8+ Rd8 Rxd8#`

Black wins a pawn for a moment and is checkmated in this line. The pawn on d2 was not free; capturing it removed the only back-rank defender. A calmer move such as 1...h6 gives the king room to breathe, and the pawn on d2 can wait for another turn.


## How to review a blitz game in five minutes

- Minute 1: Replay the game without an engine. Mark the first move that surprised you or left you unsure, rather than assuming the final blunder was the only useful moment.
- Minute 2: Stop before that move. Name your opponent's last threat and your loose pieces. Write down the move you actually considered.
- Minutes 3–4: Replay your move and the opponent's most forcing check or capture. Compare one legal alternative on the board. If you use engine analysis, ask what the suggested move prevents before copying it.
- Minute 5: Write one portable cue, such as 'check back-rank threats before my rook leaves the eighth rank.' Then try a similar position or play a free practice game.

For the rook example above, the review note is specific: 'I played 1...Rxd2 because I saw a pawn; I missed 2.Ra8+ after my rook left d8.' That note tells you what to scan next time. 'I blundered' does not. If the game record or a reliable position is unavailable, do not invent a variation from memory.


## Make it a habit

A full written review belongs after the game. During play, use the short version: what changed, what is loose, and what can they force after my move? Spend the most attention when your king is exposed or you are about to move a defender.

Run the questions in the same order every time: their move, my loose pieces, my move's consequences. On a short clock, compress them to a glance: the piece that just moved, your king, the square you are about to move to.

- Pick three positions from your last game where you hesitated.
- For each one, write the three answers in a single line.
- Find the move where, with hindsight, one of the answers would have changed your decision.

Keep the notes brief enough to revisit. A repeated cue across several games is a better practice target than a long list of unrelated mistakes.

> Question three is the one that stops the unforced blunder: imagine your move played, then list their checks and captures before you play it.
